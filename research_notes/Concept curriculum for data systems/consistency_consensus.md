## 1. The map of consistency models (Jepsen hierarchy, Viotti and Vukolic 2016): what each guarantee means as observable behaviour

### Takeaway
Consistency models are predicates over histories (what clients can observe), arranged in a partial order: linearizable implies sequential implies causal, and causal implies PRAM and writes-follow-reads, while PRAM implies read-your-writes, monotonic reads and monotonic writes. On a separate, transactional axis, strict serializable sits above both serializable and linearizable. The availability line is the most teachable fact on the map: nothing at or above sequential (or snapshot isolation or cursor stability) can stay available in a partition, causal and the session guarantees can be "sticky available" (a client that keeps talking to the same replica keeps working), and read committed and weaker can be totally available.

### Cited Findings

**The hierarchy and its sources**
- Jepsen's map has two families. The single-object family is strict serializable (shared top), linearizable, sequential, causal, writes-follow-reads, PRAM, monotonic reads, monotonic writes and read-your-writes. The multi-object (transactional) family is strict serializable, serializable, repeatable read, snapshot isolation, monotonic atomic view, cursor stability, read committed and read uncommitted. — [Jepsen consistency models](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models)
- Edges (A implies B): strict serializable to serializable and to linearizable. Linearizable to sequential to causal. Causal to writes-follow-reads and to PRAM. PRAM to monotonic reads, monotonic writes and read-your-writes. Serializable to repeatable read and to snapshot isolation. Snapshot isolation and repeatable read are incomparable. — [Jepsen consistency models](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models)
- Availability classes under partitions (asynchronous network): cursor stability, snapshot isolation, sequential and everything stronger "cannot be totally available". Read-your-writes and everything stronger (PRAM, causal) are at most sticky available. Read committed, read uncommitted and monotonic atomic view are totally available. — [Jepsen consistency models](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models)
- The Jepsen map is adapted from Bailis, Davidson, Fekete et al. (PVLDB vol. 7, the "Highly Available Transactions" paper) and from Viotti and Vukolic (arXiv:1512.00168). — [Jepsen consistency models](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models)
- Viotti and Vukolic survey "more than 50 different consistency notions", from linearizability to eventual and weak consistency, define many of them precisely, order them by semantic strength, and map them to real systems. Scope: non-transactional, single-object semantics only. The arXiv record (v4, April 2016) lists no venue; it is commonly cited as ACM Computing Surveys 2016 `[not re-read]`. — [Viotti and Vukolic, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.00168)
- Viotti and Vukolic decompose linearizability into three constraints, SingleOrder, RealTime and RVal, and sequential consistency into SingleOrder, PRAM and RVal. — [Jepsen linearizable](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models/linearizable); [Jepsen sequential](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models/sequential)

**Definitions as observable behaviour**
- Linearizable: every operation appears to take effect atomically at some point between its invocation and its completion, in an order that respects real time. If A completes before B begins, B takes effect after A. Single-object only, so it says nothing about ordering across separate objects or tables. — [Jepsen linearizable](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models/linearizable)
- Sequential (Lamport 1979): the result equals some single total order of all operations that respects each process's own program order, with no real-time constraint. A process may run far behind others and read arbitrarily stale state, but once it has seen B's operation it can never again see a state from before it. Use linearizability instead when one process learns of an event through a side channel and must then observe it. — [Jepsen sequential](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models/sequential)
- DDIA's framing: linearizability is a "recency guarantee", the illusion of a single copy with atomic operations; its other names are atomic consistency, strong consistency, immediate consistency and external consistency. — DDIA2e p.402-403/PDF 426-427
- DDIA's motivating history (Figure 10-1): Aaliyah reloads and sees the final score; she tells Bryce; Bryce reloads afterwards, hits a lagging replica, and sees the game still in progress. The violation is only detectable because of a second channel (her voice). — DDIA2e p.403-404/PDF 427-428
- Session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads, monotonic writes, writes-follow-reads) come from Terry et al., "Session Guarantees for Weakly Consistent Replicated Data", PDIS 1994 `[not re-read]` ([DOI 10.1109/PDIS.1994.331722](https://doi.org/10.1109/PDIS.1994.331722)). DDIA presents read-after-write, monotonic reads and consistent prefix reads as replication-lag fixes, and notes linearizability includes all of them. — DDIA2e p.407/PDF 431
- MongoDB documents the practical cost of session guarantees: causally consistent sessions guarantee causal consistency only when reads use `majority` read concern and writes use `majority` write concern; the default read concern `local` can return data that is later rolled back. — [MongoDB defaults](https://www.mongodb.com/docs/manual/reference/mongodb-defaults/)
- Eventual consistency (DDIA's framing): one of two philosophies; replication is made visible to the application, which must handle inconsistencies and conflicts. Inevitable for apps that accept offline writes. — DDIA2e p.401-402/PDF 425-426

**Strict serializability vs serializability vs linearizability, with histories**
- Serializability is a transaction isolation property over multiple objects: transactions behave as if executed in some serial order, which may differ from the real order. Linearizability is a single-register recency property and does not prevent write skew. Serializability permits stale reads. Both together are strict serializability (strong one-copy serializability, strong-1SR). — DDIA2e p.407-408/PDF 431-432
- Concrete product statements: CockroachDB offers serializability plus some recency guarantees, not strict serializability; Spanner and FoundationDB offer strict serializability. Consistency model and isolation level can be chosen largely independently. — DDIA2e p.408/PDF 432
- Spanner calls its strict-serializable guarantee "external consistency": the system behaves as if transactions ran sequentially, and if one transaction finishes before another starts committing, no client sees the second's effects without the first's. Google's docs say this is stronger than linearizability, which covers single-object reads and writes. — [Spanner TrueTime and external consistency](https://docs.cloud.google.com/spanner/docs/true-time-external-consistency)
- Kleppmann (2015): PostgreSQL's SSI gives serializability but not linearizability, and Oracle (snapshot isolation sold as "serializable") gives neither. — [Kleppmann, Please stop calling databases CP or AP](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2015/05/11/please-stop-calling-databases-cp-or-ap.html)

**Concept card: consistency models (problem / simplest version / where it breaks / improvement)**
- Problem: once data has more than one copy, "what will a read return?" has many answers; the application needs a contract. Simplest version: "behave like one copy" (linearizable) — DDIA2e p.402/PDF 426. Where it breaks: it costs a round trip to a quorum or leader on every operation and becomes unavailable on the minority side of a partition ([Jepsen models](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models)). Improvement: pick the weakest model the invariant tolerates; causal plus session guarantees stay sticky-available ([Jepsen models](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models)), and products expose the choice per request (see section 6).

**Misconceptions to name**
- "Strong consistency" is a single thing. DDIA says the phrase is vague and replaces it with linearizability. — DDIA2e p.402/PDF 426
- "Serializable implies up to date." Serializability allows stale reads. — DDIA2e p.408/PDF 432
- "Sequential consistency is the strongest model." etcd's own docs once claimed this; Jepsen flagged it as wrong and said etcd's definition of linearizability conflicted with Herlihy and Wing. — [Jepsen etcd 3.4.3](https://jepsen.io/analyses/etcd-3.4.3)

### Inferences
- Teach the map with two-to-four-operation register histories, one per edge: (a) sequential but not linearizable: client A writes x=1 and gets OK; afterwards B reads x=0 (legal if B's read is ordered before A's write; illegal under real time). (b) causal but not sequential: two clients write x=1 and x=2 concurrently; replica R1 shows 1 then 2, R2 shows 2 then 1 (no single order, but no causality is violated). (c) PRAM but not causal: B reads A's post "question" and writes "answer"; C sees "answer" before "question". (d) read-your-writes failure: write to leader, then read from a lagging follower returns the old value. These are standard textbook constructions derived from the definitions above, not taken from a single source.
- Present "eventual consistency" as a liveness promise only (replicas converge if writes stop) with no safety content; this is why it sits at the bottom and why the session guarantees exist. This is an inference from the hierarchy plus DDIA's framing.
- Keep the two axes visually separate (single-object vs transactional) and show strict serializable as the join. Learners routinely conflate "serializable" and "linearizable" because both say "some sequential order"; the DDIA history (stale read allowed by serializability) is the cleanest counterexample.

## 2. Linearizability (Herlihy and Wing 1990): register diagrams, checking, what needs it, which replication provides it, and what it costs (CAP, PACELC, multicore)

### Takeaway
Linearizability means each operation takes effect at one instant inside its own request-to-response interval, so once any client has seen a new value nobody may see an older one. It is required wherever correctness depends on "exactly one" (leader, lock holder, username) or on a second channel carrying timing (queue message beats replication). Single-leader replication can provide it if the leader truly is the leader; consensus provides it if reads are also checked; quorums alone do not (a concrete three-replica history breaks it). Its cost is fundamental: unavailability on the minority side of a partition (CAP, as Gilbert and Lynch formalized it, a narrow theorem) and, more importantly every day, latency proportional to network delay uncertainty (Attiya and Welch; PACELC's "else latency"). Even multicore RAM drops it for speed.

### Cited Findings

**Definition and register diagrams**
- Original paper: Herlihy and Wing, "Linearizability: A Correctness Condition for Concurrent Objects", ACM TOPLAS 12(3), 1990 `[not re-read]` ([DOI 10.1145/78969.78972](https://doi.org/10.1145/78969.78972)); Jepsen credits it as the formal source. — [Jepsen linearizable](https://jepsen.io/consistency/models/linearizable)
- DDIA's diagram vocabulary: a "register" is one key, row or document; each request is a bar from send to response; the database processed it somewhere inside the bar. Reads that overlap a write may return old or new. — DDIA2e p.404-405/PDF 428-429
- The extra constraint that makes it linearizable (Figure 10-3): once any read returns the new value, every later read (by real time, any client) must return it, even while the write is still in flight. — DDIA2e p.405/PDF 429
- Figure 10-4's worked history adds CAS(x, old, new). Linearization points joined in order must always move forward in time. Details: B's read can return a value written by requests sent after B's (concurrent requests can be reordered); B can see 1 before A gets its OK; D's CAS fails because x is no longer 0; B's final read of 2 is illegal because A already read 4 before B's read began. — DDIA2e p.406-407/PDF 430-431

**How to check a history**
- DDIA: checking is possible but computationally expensive; record request and response timings and search for a valid sequential order. — DDIA2e p.407/PDF 431
- Testing linearizability of a history is NP-complete in general (Gibbons and Korach, "Testing Shared Memories", SIAM J. Comput. 26(4), 1997 `[not re-read]`; [DOI 10.1137/S0097539794279614](https://doi.org/10.1137/S0097539794279614)). Elle's README states the related result that serializability checking is NP-complete, which is why Elle limits itself to linear or log-linear inferences. — [Elle](https://github.com/jepsen-io/elle)
- Knossos (Jepsen's linearizability checker) blows up exponentially with concurrency and is often limited to a few hundred operations per history; Elle (transactional, list-append and rw-register workloads) is roughly linear in history length and handles hundreds of thousands of operations, and can use real-time order to distinguish strict serializable from serializable. — [Elle](https://github.com/jepsen-io/elle)
- Porcupine (Go) implements "Faster linearizability checking via P-compositionality", an optimization of Lowe's "Testing for Linearizability" (itself building on Wing and Gong). Models are a sequential spec (`Init`, `Step(state, input, output) -> (ok, newState)`, optional `Hash`); histories can be split into independent partitions (e.g. one per key). It is "generally 1,000x to 10,000x faster" than Knossos on Jepsen histories and produces an interactive HTML visualization of linearization points and the longest partial linearization when it fails. Users include etcd's robustness tests, Amazon MemoryDB (SIGMOD'24) and MIT 6.5840's Raft KV lab. — [Porcupine](https://github.com/anishathalye/porcupine)
- JetBrains Lincheck (JVM, Kotlin-friendly) generates concurrent scenarios from `@Operation` methods and checks results "linearizability by default", in stress mode (`StressOptions`) or bounded model checking (`ModelCheckingOptions`), with reproducible traces in IntelliJ. Latest per README: `org.jetbrains.lincheck:lincheck:3.7`. — [Lincheck](https://github.com/JetBrains/lincheck)

**What needs linearizability**
- Locking and leader election: a lease-based leader election must be linearizable; two nodes must not acquire the lease at once (split brain). ZooKeeper and etcd implement leases via consensus; Apache Curator provides recipes. — DDIA2e p.408-409/PDF 432-433
- Uniqueness constraints (username, file path), "balance never negative", "don't oversell stock", "one person per seat" require a single up-to-date value all nodes agree on; foreign-key or attribute constraints do not. Loosely enforced constraints (overbook then compensate) can skip it. — DDIA2e p.409-410/PDF 433-434
- Cross-channel timing dependencies (Figure 10-5): a web server stores a video in file storage, then enqueues a transcode job; if storage is not linearizable, the queue can beat storage replication and the transcoder reads an old or missing file, leaving original and transcode permanently inconsistent. Same race: push notification arrives before the data fetch can see the data. — DDIA2e p.410-411/PDF 434-435
- Linearizable ID generation: DDIA Figure 10-10, a user sets the account private on a laptop then uploads a photo from a phone; with Lamport or hybrid clocks on two shards, the photo can get a lower timestamp than the privacy change and an MVCC snapshot read shows the photo publicly. A linearizable ID generator (single-node counter, replicated; TiDB/TiKV's "timestamp oracle" from Percolator) fixes it; Spanner instead waits out clock uncertainty. — DDIA2e p.423-425/PDF 447-449

**Which replication schemes provide it**
- Single-leader: potentially linearizable if all reads and writes go to the leader and you know for sure who the leader is; a "delusional" leader violates it, and async failover can lose committed writes. Sharding with one leader per shard does not affect it (single-object guarantee). — DDIA2e p.411/PDF 435
- Consensus: "likely linearizable" (ZooKeeper uses Zab, etcd uses Raft), but reads served by a node that doesn't check it is still leader can be stale. — DDIA2e p.412/PDF 436
- Multi-leader: not linearizable. Leaderless: probably not; LWW on time-of-day clocks (Cassandra, ScyllaDB) is almost certainly not. — DDIA2e p.412/PDF 436
- Quorum counterexample (Figure 10-6): n=3, a writer sends x=1 to all three (w=3); concurrently client A reads a quorum of two and gets {1, 0}, returning 1; afterwards client B reads a different two and gets {0, 0}, returning 0. w + r > n holds, yet B started after A finished and saw older data. — DDIA2e p.412-413/PDF 436-437
- Fix and its limit: synchronous read repair before returning, plus a read-before-write to pick a greater timestamp, makes Dynamo-style registers linearizable (this is the ABD construction); Riak skips synchronous read repair for performance; Cassandra waits for read repair on quorum reads but loses linearizability through wall-clock timestamps. A linearizable CAS cannot be built this way at all; it needs consensus. — DDIA2e p.412-413/PDF 436-437
- Further quorum edge cases: a node with the new value fails and is restored from a stale replica; rebalancing makes quorums stop overlapping; a write that succeeded on fewer than w nodes is reported failed but not rolled back, so later reads may or may not see it; LWW clock skew silently drops writes. — DDIA2e p.233-234/PDF 257-258
- Cassandra's own docs: QUORUM is n/2+1; with RF=3, QUORUM reads and writes overlap on at least one replica, "W + R > RF"; read repair and hinted handoff are best effort; every mutation carries a client or coordinator timestamp and the latest wins, so clocks must be synced. — [Cassandra Dynamo architecture](https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/architecture/dynamo.html)
- Abadi: "simply using a quorum protocol is not sufficient" to get Gilbert and Lynch's consistency. — [Abadi, PACELC, IEEE Computer Feb 2012](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~abadi/papers/abadi-pacelc.pdf)

**Cost 1: CAP as Brewer stated it and as Gilbert and Lynch proved it**
- Brewer named CAP in 2000 as a rule of thumb without precise definitions; the trade-off was known to database designers since the 1970s. CAP pushed engineers toward shared-nothing designs and helped trigger NoSQL. — DDIA2e p.415/PDF 439
- Gilbert and Lynch's formal version ("Brewer's Conjecture and the Feasibility of Consistent, Available, Partition-Tolerant Web Services", SIGACT News 2002 `[not re-read]`; [PDF](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~adrian/731-sp04/readings/GL-cap.pdf)) uses: consistency = linearizability of a single read/write register; availability = every request received by a non-failing node gets a (non-error) response; partition = the network may drop arbitrarily many messages. Kleppmann's summary of those definitions: — [Kleppmann 2015](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2015/05/11/please-stop-calling-databases-cp-or-ap.html)
- DDIA: formal CAP considers one model (linearizability) and one fault (partitions, which Google data says cause under 8% of incidents); it says nothing about delays, dead nodes or other trade-offs, has "little practical value", and has been superseded by more precise results. — DDIA2e p.415/PDF 439
- "Pick two of three" is misleading because partitions are not chosen; a better phrasing is "either consistent or available when partitioned". CAP's availability does not match the everyday meaning, and some systems are neither CP nor AP. — DDIA2e p.416/PDF 440 (sidebar "The Unhelpful CAP Theorem")
- Brewer 2012 ("CAP Twelve Years Later", IEEE Computer, republished by InfoQ 30 May 2012): partitions are rare, so C and A are usually both achievable; CAP prohibits "only a tiny part of the design space"; manage partitions explicitly in three steps (detect, enter a partition mode that limits operations and logs, recover and compensate); in practice a partition is a timeout, so tighter time bounds mean entering partition mode more often; ATMs keep dispensing during partitions but cap net withdrawals (e.g. $200) and compensate later. — [Brewer 2012 via InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/articles/cap-twelve-years-later-how-the-rules-have-changed/)
- Kleppmann's case analysis: single-leader databases are neither CAP-consistent (async follower reads are stale) nor CAP-available (a client cut off from the leader can't write); ZooKeeper by default serves reads from one node (not linearizable), needs a majority for writes (not CAP-available), so it is "really just P", yet gives strong, useful guarantees; MongoDB is single-leader per shard and Jepsen showed stale reads at its highest setting. Recommendation: stop using CP/AP labels and reason about the specific guarantees and latency. — [Kleppmann 2015](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2015/05/11/please-stop-calling-databases-cp-or-ap.html)

**Cost 2: latency even without partitions (PACELC, Attiya and Welch)**
- PACELC (Abadi, IEEE Computer, February 2012): if Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency. The latency-consistency trade-off exists during normal operation because of replication and is "magnified" over a WAN. Classification: default Dynamo, Cassandra, Riak are PA/EL; VoltDB/H-Store and Megastore are PC/EC; BigTable/HBase are PC/EC; MongoDB (2012) PA/EC; PNUTS PC/EL. A consistent read option can cost "a factor of four or more" in latency in a cited study. — [Abadi PACELC](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~abadi/papers/abadi-pacelc.pdf)
- Abadi's MongoDB label is a period piece: MongoDB now defaults to `w: "majority"`. — [MongoDB defaults](https://www.mongodb.com/docs/manual/reference/mongodb-defaults/)
- DDIA notes PACELC inherits CAP's counterintuitive definitions. — DDIA2e p.415/PDF 439
- Attiya and Welch prove that linearizable reads and writes have response time at least proportional to network delay uncertainty; no faster algorithm exists; weaker models can be much faster. Many databases drop linearizability for performance, not fault tolerance. — DDIA2e p.417/PDF 441 (citing Attiya and Welch, "Sequential Consistency versus Linearizability", ACM TOCS 1994 `[not re-read]`)
- Measured local cost (etcd 3.2 benchmark, 3 members on GCE, 8-byte keys, 256-byte values): one client, linearizable reads 1,353 QPS at 0.7 ms vs serializable 2,909 QPS at 0.3 ms; 1,000 clients over 100 connections, linearizable 141,578 QPS at 5.5 ms vs serializable 185,758 QPS at 2.2 ms. — [etcd performance](https://etcd.io/docs/v3.6/op-guide/performance/)
- Measured WAN cost: DynamoDB MRSC global tables say write and strongly consistent read latency depend on the three Regions chosen because they require cross-Region communication; MREC has lower latency. — [DynamoDB global tables](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazondynamodb/latest/developerguide/V2globaltables_HowItWorks.html)

**Cost 3: why multicore memory isn't linearizable**
- On a multicore CPU a write by one core may not be visible to a read on another shortly after unless a memory barrier/fence is used, because each core has its own cache and store buffer flushed asynchronously. The reason is performance, not fault tolerance, so invoking CAP here makes no sense. — DDIA2e p.416-417/PDF 440-441

**Named incidents and numbers for "where it breaks"**
- GitHub, 21 October 2018: a 43-second loss of connectivity between the US East Coast hub and the primary East Coast data center let Orchestrator (Raft-based) form a quorum with West Coast and cloud nodes and fail MySQL primaries over to the West Coast. Several seconds of East Coast writes (954 on the busiest cluster) had not replicated; West Coast accepted writes for nearly 40 minutes; neither side could be discarded, so GitHub failed forward and ran degraded for 24 hours 11 minutes; about 200,000 webhook payloads expired. Lesson: stop cross-region promotion; cross-country latency was a major factor. — [GitHub post-incident analysis](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/oct21-post-incident-analysis/)
- MongoDB 4.2.6 (Jepsen 2020): with weak write concern, acknowledged writes were lost under partitions; read skew in transactions; advice: set read and write concern explicitly. — [Jepsen MongoDB 4.2.6](https://jepsen.io/analyses/mongodb-4.2.6)

**Misconceptions**
- "CAP: pick any two." (DDIA2e p.416/PDF 440; Brewer 2012.)
- "CAP's C is ACID's C." It is linearizability. — [Kleppmann 2015](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2015/05/11/please-stop-calling-databases-cp-or-ap.html)
- "R + W > N means strongly consistent." Figure 10-6. — DDIA2e p.412-413/PDF 436-437
- "Uses consensus, therefore all reads are linearizable." ZooKeeper default reads and Consul `default` mode are counterexamples (see section 5). — DDIA2e p.412/PDF 436
- "The trade-off only bites during partitions." Attiya-Welch and PACELC: it bites on every request. — DDIA2e p.417/PDF 441; [Abadi](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~abadi/papers/abadi-pacelc.pdf)

### Inferences
- The strongest pedagogical sequence: (1) draw bars; (2) let learners place linearization points by hand on DDIA-style histories; (3) give them a checker; (4) make them produce a failing history from a real or simulated system (Figure 10-6 quorum race) and a passing one after the fix (synchronous read repair plus read-before-write). This keeps CAP until after they have felt the latency cost.
- Teach CAP last and briefly, as Gilbert and Lynch's narrow theorem, then pivot immediately to PACELC and Attiya-Welch as "the trade-off you pay every day". The etcd 0.7 ms vs 0.3 ms numbers make the "E" in PACELC tangible on one machine.
- The GitHub incident is better framed as "consensus elected a leader correctly, but the system above it was not designed for the outcome" than as a CAP story, which is a useful corrective.

## 3. Ordering, causality, total order broadcast, the consensus equivalences, and FLP (and how real systems escape it)

### Takeaway
Ordering tools form a ladder: Lamport clocks give a total order consistent with causality but not recency; hybrid logical clocks add closeness to wall time; vector clocks detect concurrency; only a linearizable ID generator or a consensus-backed log gives real-time order. Consensus is one problem in many disguises: single-value agreement, linearizable compare-and-set, a shared append-only log (total order / atomic broadcast) and fault-tolerant atomic commit are mutually reducible, while fetch-and-add is weaker (consensus number 2). FLP says no deterministic algorithm can guarantee termination in a fully asynchronous system with even one crash; real systems escape by keeping safety unconditional and buying liveness with timeouts (partial synchrony), failure detectors or randomness.

### Cited Findings

**Causality and logical clocks**
- Requirements for a logical clock: compact unique timestamps, totally ordered, consistent with causality (if A happened before B, ts(A) < ts(B)). Distributed ID schemes (sharded ranges, preallocated blocks, UUIDv4, UUIDv7/Snowflake/ULID/ObjectID) give uniqueness but not causal order. — DDIA2e p.418-420/PDF 442-444
- Lamport timestamps (Lamport 1978): pairs (counter, nodeID); increment on each event; on receipt, raise the counter to the max seen. In DDIA's chat example the order is (1,"Aaliyah") < (1,"Caleb") < (2,"Bryce"). Lamport clocks give a total order but not linearizability. — DDIA2e p.420-421/PDF 444-445. Original: "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System", CACM 21(7), 1978 `[not re-read]` ([DOI 10.1145/359545.359563](https://doi.org/10.1145/359545.359563))
- Hybrid logical clocks combine physical time with Lamport's max rule and always increment, staying monotonic across NTP jumps; used by CockroachDB. Lamport and HLC timestamps order concurrent events arbitrarily, so you cannot tell concurrency from them; vector clocks can, at the cost of one counter per node. — DDIA2e p.421-422/PDF 445-446
- Why logical clocks can't enforce uniqueness: picking the lowest timestamp as the lock winner requires hearing from every node that might hold a lower one; one unreachable node halts the system. "We need consensus." — DDIA2e p.425/PDF 449

**The many faces of consensus (equivalences)**
- Consensus properties: uniform agreement, integrity, validity (safety) and termination (liveness). Without fault tolerance a hard-coded "dictator" satisfies the first three; termination is what makes it hard. Any consensus algorithm needs a functioning majority to terminate, but good algorithms keep the safety properties even if a majority fails. — DDIA2e p.427-429/PDF 451-453
- CAS ⇔ consensus: initialise a register to null; each proposer does CAS(null, myValue); the stored value is the decision. Conversely, consensus decides among CAS attempts with the same expected value. — DDIA2e p.429/PDF 453
- Shared log (total order broadcast / atomic broadcast) ⇔ consensus: properties are eventual append, reliable delivery, append-only, agreement and validity; decide the first log entry; conversely run one consensus instance per log slot, filling slots in order. Single-leader replication without failover fails liveness. — DDIA2e p.429-431/PDF 453-455
- Fetch-and-add is weaker: it solves consensus for two proposers only (consensus number 2), while CAS and shared logs have consensus number infinity. — DDIA2e p.431-432/PDF 455-456. Consensus numbers originate in Herlihy, "Wait-Free Synchronization", ACM TOPLAS 13(1), 1991 `[not re-read]` ([DOI 10.1145/114005.102808](https://doi.org/10.1145/114005.102808))
- Atomic commitment differs from consensus (must abort if anyone votes abort; nontriviality) but fault-tolerant atomic commit and consensus are inter-reducible: everyone broadcasts votes, then runs consensus on commit/abort; or use atomic commit over CAS transactions to decide a value. 2PC alone has a single-point-of-failure coordinator. — DDIA2e p.432-433/PDF 456-457
- In practice most consensus systems expose a shared log: Raft, VR and Zab natively; Paxos decides single values but production systems use Multi-Paxos for a log. The log gives state machine replication, event sourcing, and serializable transactions via deterministic stored procedures. A per-shard log scales but limits cross-shard guarantees. — DDIA2e p.433-434/PDF 457-458
- A log counter yields fencing tokens; in ZooKeeper this sequence number is the zxid. — DDIA2e p.434/PDF 458

**FLP and escaping it**
- FLP (Fischer, Lynch and Paterson, JACM 1985 `[not re-read]`, [DOI 10.1145/3149.214121](https://doi.org/10.1145/3149.214121)) proves no deterministic algorithm always reaches consensus if a node may crash, in the asynchronous model (no clocks or timeouts). It does not say consensus is never reached, only that termination cannot be guaranteed. With timeouts used to suspect crashes (even wrongly) consensus becomes solvable; randomness also suffices. — DDIA2e p.426/PDF 450
- The timeout escape is formalized by partial synchrony (Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer, JACM 1988 `[not re-read]`, [DOI 10.1145/42282.42283](https://doi.org/10.1145/42282.42283)) and unreliable failure detectors (Chandra and Toueg, JACM 1996 `[not re-read]`, [DOI 10.1145/226643.226647](https://doi.org/10.1145/226643.226647)); DDIA cites these as its references [75]. — DDIA2e p.426/PDF 450
- Paxos Made Simple states the same pattern from the algorithm side: two proposers can each keep completing phase 1 with higher numbers so neither's phase 2 succeeds (dueling proposers); progress requires a distinguished proposer, and electing one reliably requires randomness or real time, which Lamport ties to the FLP result. — [Lamport, Paxos Made Simple, 1 Nov 2001](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/paxos-simple.pdf)
- Raft's concrete escape: randomized election timeouts (e.g. 150-300 ms) and the timing requirement broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF; safety never depends on timing. — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)

**Concept card: ordering and consensus**
- Problem: replicas must agree on one order (or one value) despite crashes and delays. Simplest version: one leader assigns sequence numbers (DDIA2e p.434/PDF 458). Breaks: leader crash means no termination; manual failover means minutes of downtime; automatic failover without consensus means split brain (DDIA2e p.434/PDF 458; GitHub 2018 incident above). Improvement: epochs plus quorum votes (section 4).
- Problem: assign IDs whose order matches real time. Simplest version: single-node counter. Breaks: single point of failure, cross-region round trip, throughput bottleneck. Improvement: batch-allocate IDs from a replicated counter (skips IDs on failover, never duplicates or reorders), or Spanner commit-wait. — DDIA2e p.418, 424/PDF 442, 448

**Misconceptions**
- "FLP means consensus is impossible in practice." — DDIA2e p.426/PDF 450
- "Lamport timestamps tell you what's latest." They give a causal total order, not recency. — DDIA2e p.420/PDF 444
- "Two rounds of voting in consensus is just 2PC." In consensus any node can start an election and a quorum suffices; in 2PC only the coordinator asks and needs every yes. — DDIA2e p.435/PDF 459

### Inferences
- The equivalences are best taught as a "reduction game": give learners a working linearizable CAS register (e.g. etcd `Txn` with `CreateRevision == 0`) and have them build a log, a lock and a unique-username service on it, then show fetch-and-add cannot decide for three proposers. This makes "consensus is one problem in many disguises" concrete before any Paxos.
- Teach FLP as a safety/liveness split: every protocol in section 4 is designed so that bad timing can only stall it, never make it disagree. Cloudflare 2020 (section 4) is the real-world stall.

### Gaps
- The FLP, DLS and Chandra-Toueg papers were not re-read; claims rest on DDIA's paraphrase and standard knowledge.
- Kafka's KRaft is a concrete total-order-broadcast product (section 5); no source fetched that discusses "Kafka topic partition as total order broadcast" with non-KRaft replication (ISR) in these terms.

## 4. Algorithms: Paxos, Multi-Paxos, Viewstamped Replication, Zab, Raft, Flexible Paxos, EPaxos, leases and read paths, reconfiguration, and their costs

### Takeaway
All the practical algorithms share one skeleton: an epoch number (ballot, view, term) guarantees at most one leader per epoch; a quorum vote installs a leader; another quorum vote commits each log entry; the two quorums must intersect. Paxos is the single-decree core (prepare/promise, accept/accepted); Multi-Paxos runs phase 1 once and pays one round trip per command; Raft repackages this with strong leadership, an up-to-date-log election rule and a current-term commit rule. Flexible Paxos shows only phase-1/phase-2 intersection matters. EPaxos removes the leader for non-conflicting commands, with better median WAN latency but worse tails and a correctness history. The costs are a strict majority, one quorum round trip per write, no write scaling with more nodes, timeout tuning, linearizable reads needing their own quorum check or a clock-dependent lease, and reconfiguration that is easy to get wrong.

### Cited Findings

**The common skeleton (DDIA's framing)**
- Consensus algorithms don't guarantee one leader at a time; they guarantee a unique leader per epoch (ballot in Paxos, view in VR, term in Raft). A node that times out starts an election with a higher epoch; higher epoch wins. Before appending, a leader collects votes from a quorum; quorums for election and for proposals must overlap. — DDIA2e p.434-435/PDF 458-459
- New-leader catch-up differs: Raft only elects a node whose log is at least as up to date as a majority's; Paxos lets any node lead but makes it bring its log up to date first. — DDIA2e p.435-436/PDF 459-460
- Weakening for availability (sidebar): Kafka's unclean leader election lets an out-of-date replica lead; async replication can't guarantee an up-to-date follower; then "the theory of consensus no longer applies". — DDIA2e p.436/PDF 460
- Consensus is "single-leader replication done right". — DDIA2e p.437/PDF 461. Any automatic failover not using a proven consensus algorithm is likely unsafe. — DDIA2e p.437/PDF 461
- Algorithms in DDIA's list: Viewstamped Replication, Paxos, Raft, Zab, all non-Byzantine (crash-recovery, arbitrary delay and loss); BFT variants assume under one-third Byzantine nodes. — DDIA2e p.426/PDF 450

**Paxos (Lamport 1998/2001) and Multi-Paxos**
- Original: "The Part-Time Parliament", ACM TOCS 16(2), 1998 `[not re-read]` ([DOI 10.1145/279227.279229](https://doi.org/10.1145/279227.279229)). Restatement: "Paxos Made Simple", dated 1 November 2001. — [Paxos Made Simple](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/paxos-simple.pdf)
- Phase 1: (a) a proposer picks number n and sends prepare(n) to a majority of acceptors; (b) an acceptor that has not responded to a higher-numbered prepare promises not to accept proposals below n and returns the highest-numbered proposal it has accepted. Phase 2: (a) with promises from a majority, the proposer sends accept(n, v) where v is the value of the highest-numbered proposal among responses, or any value if none; (b) an acceptor accepts unless it has promised a higher number. — [Paxos Made Simple](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/paxos-simple.pdf)
- Multi-Paxos as described there: a leader runs phase 1 once for all future instances, then each client command needs only phase 2; gaps (e.g. instances 136-137 unknown after a leader change) are filled with no-op commands so later commands (138-140) can execute. — [Paxos Made Simple](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/paxos-simple.pdf)
- Liveness: dueling proposers can livelock; a distinguished proposer (leader) is required for progress. — [Paxos Made Simple](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/paxos-simple.pdf)
- Round trips (derived from the phases): classic single-decree Paxos = 2 round trips from proposer to a majority (prepare, accept); steady-state Multi-Paxos = 1 round trip per command. Cassandra's LWT, which runs Paxos per partition, is cited by third parties as 4 round trips in Paxos v1 vs 1 for a normal write (prepare, read, propose, commit). — [AxonOps LWT docs](https://docs.axonops.com/data-platforms/cassandra/cql/dml/lightweight-transactions) (third-party; Apache docs do not state the count)
- Engineering reality: "Paxos Made Live" (Chandra, Griesemer, Redstone, PODC 2007 `[not re-read]`, [DOI 10.1145/1281100.1281103](https://doi.org/10.1145/1281100.1281103)) is the standard account of the gap between the algorithm and a production Chubby implementation.

**Viewstamped Replication and Zab**
- VR (Oki and Liskov, PODC 1988 `[not re-read]`, [DOI 10.1145/62546.62549](https://doi.org/10.1145/62546.62549); "Viewstamped Replication Revisited", Liskov and Cowling, MIT-CSAIL-TR-2012-021 `[not re-read]`, [PDF](http://pmg.csail.mit.edu/papers/vr-revisited.pdf)) is a log-replication protocol with views (epochs) and view changes; Raft's authors call Oki and Liskov's VR the algorithm Raft most resembles. — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)
- Zab (Junqueira, Reed, Serafini, DSN 2011 `[not re-read]`, [DOI 10.1109/DSN.2011.5958223](https://doi.org/10.1109/DSN.2011.5958223)) is ZooKeeper's atomic broadcast; DDIA names it alongside Raft as the consensus beneath ZooKeeper. — DDIA2e p.412/PDF 436

**Raft (Ongaro and Ousterhout, USENIX ATC 2014; extended version 20 May 2014)**
- Five safety properties (Figure 3): Election Safety (at most one leader per term), Leader Append-Only, Log Matching (same index and term implies identical prefix), Leader Completeness (a committed entry is in every later leader's log), State Machine Safety (no two servers apply different commands at the same index). — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)
- Commit rule (Figure 8): an entry from an earlier term can be stored on a majority and still be overwritten by a future leader. So Raft only commits entries from the leader's current term by counting replicas; earlier entries commit indirectly via Log Matching. — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)
- Timing: broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF; broadcast time 0.5-20 ms depending on storage, so election timeouts of roughly 10-500 ms; server MTBFs are months. Recommended: randomized 150-300 ms. — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)
- Measured elections (5 servers, worst-case log lengths, synchronized heartbeats before crash): without randomness elections consistently took over 10 seconds (split votes); 5 ms of randomness gave median downtime 287 ms; 50 ms of randomness gave worst case 513 ms over 1,000 trials; with 12-24 ms timeouts the average was 35 ms (longest 152 ms) but going lower violates the timing requirement. — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)
- Membership change via joint consensus (C_old,new needs majorities of both), plus: new servers join as non-voting until caught up; a leader not in C_new steps down once C_new commits; removed servers would disrupt with higher terms, so servers ignore RequestVote within the minimum election timeout of hearing from a leader. Switching directly from old to new config is unsafe (two leaders in one term, Figure 10). — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)
- Single-server membership changes (from Ongaro's thesis) had a safety bug announced on raft-dev on 10 July 2015: concurrent single-server changes across term boundaries, starting from an even-sized cluster, can produce non-overlapping quorums and split brain; joint consensus unaffected. Fix: a new leader must commit an entry (its no-op) in its current term before applying membership changes. Ongaro noted no formal proof existed for either membership scheme at that time. Found by CMU students formalizing the protocol. — [raft-dev thread](https://groups.google.com/g/raft-dev/c/t4xj6dJTP6E); [Notes on Raft's reconfiguration bug (2024)](https://will62794.github.io/distributed-systems/2024/02/09/note-on-raft-reconfig-bug.html)
- Linearizable reads in the paper: the leader commits a blank no-op at the start of its term to learn the commit index, and exchanges heartbeats with a majority before answering a read; alternatively use heartbeats as a lease, which relies on bounded clock skew for safety. — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)
- Understandability study: 43 students at two universities; 33 did better on Raft; mean Raft score 25.7 vs Paxos 20.8 out of 60; 15 had prior Paxos experience. Original implementation: about 2,000 lines of C++ in RAMCloud. — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)
- Known edge cases: with one consistently unreliable link, Raft leadership can bounce between two nodes or the leader can be forced to resign repeatedly; a pre-vote phase was added; EPaxos-style leaderless protocols are more robust to slow nodes. — DDIA2e p.437/PDF 461

**Read paths: ReadIndex and leases**
- etcd-io/raft supports linearizable read-only queries two ways: ReadIndex (leader confirms leadership with a quorum, bypasses the log; followers ask the leader for a safe read index) and lease-based (leader serves locally, depends on clocks). It also has leadership transfer, automatic step-down when quorum is lost, batching, pipelining and flow control. It leaves network and disk I/O to the user; Ready loop order: persist Entries, HardState, Snapshot, then send messages (no messages before HardState is durable), then apply, then `Advance()`. Users: etcd, Kubernetes, CockroachDB, TiKV, Docker Swarm, dgraph and others. — [etcd-io/raft](https://github.com/etcd-io/raft)
- etcd 3.7's raft v3.7.0 hardens ReadIndex against stale reads by adding a unique identifier to the heartbeat context for read-only requests. — [Announcing etcd v3.7.0](https://etcd.io/blog/2026/announcing-etcd-3.7/)
- Chubby used a master lease: replicas promise not to elect another master for a few seconds; reads are served by the master alone, safe while the lease is valid. — [Chubby, OSDI 2006](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/chubby-osdi06.pdf)
- DDIA: linearizable reads must also go through a quorum to confirm leadership; etcd does this. — DDIA2e p.436/PDF 460

**Flexible Paxos (Howard, Malkhi, Spiegelman, arXiv 24 Aug 2016)**
- Majority quorums are not necessary; intersection is required only between phase-1 (leader election) and phase-2 (replication) quorums. Examples: shrink phase-2 quorums by one when acceptor count is even; use small disjoint phase-2 quorums to speed steady state. The abstract gives no quantitative results. — [Flexible Paxos](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.06696)
- DDIA notes quorums are "typically, but not always" majorities, citing this line of work. — DDIA2e p.435/PDF 459

**EPaxos (Moraru, Andersen, Kaminsky, SOSP 2013) and its afterlife**
- Goals: optimal WAN commit latency for 1-2 failures, uniform load across replicas, graceful degradation with slow replicas; needs only a simple majority non-faulty; commits in one round trip in the common case and at most two otherwise. Non-interfering commands commit on the fast path with a fast quorum of F + ⌊(F+1)/2⌋ replicas including the command leader; interfering commands acquire dependencies and may need a slow path with a classic quorum of F+1. — [EPaxos paper](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dga/papers/epaxos-sosp2013.pdf)
- Correctness bug (Sutra, arXiv 1906.10917, 2019; IPL 2020): in both the TLA+ spec and the Go implementation, a single ballot variable per replica lets replicas diverge on a command's dependencies, breaking linearizability; the proposed fix (track the last ballot voted) was not proven at the time. — [Sutra, On the correctness of Egalitarian Paxos](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1906.10917)
- EPaxos Revisited (Tollman, Park, Ousterhout, NSDI 2021): median WAN latency claim confirmed, but tail latency more than 4x worse than Multi-Paxos; synchronized clocks cut conflicts by at least 50% and mean latency by up to 7.5%. — [EPaxos Revisited](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi21/presentation/tollman)
- 2025: Ryabinin, Gotsman and Sutra (OPODIS 2025) present a simplified, corrected EPaxos and report a further bug where recovery can deadlock. — [Making Democracy Work, OPODIS 2025](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol361-opodis2025/LIPIcs.OPODIS.2025.22/LIPIcs.OPODIS.2025.22.pdf)
- Cassandra 6.0's Accord is a leaderless consensus protocol giving strict-serializable multi-partition transactions, opt-in per table. — [Cassandra 6 status, July 2026 (blog)](https://www.youngju.dev/blog/2026-07-17-cassandra-6-accord-alpha1-status.en)

**Costs and numbers**
- Strict majority: 3 nodes tolerate 1 failure, 5 tolerate 2; every operation needs a quorum, so adding nodes slows it; only the majority side of a partition progresses; timeouts are hard to tune across regions (too long = slow recovery, too short = election storms). — DDIA2e p.437/PDF 461
- Kafka's KRaft docs state the same rule: 2N+1 controllers to survive N failures; plan 3 or 5. — [Kafka 4.4 KRaft operations](https://kafka.apache.org/44/operations/kraft/)
- Write throughput falls as replicas are added: ZooKeeper (2010 paper, saturated) 3 servers 87k reads/s or 21k writes/s; 5 servers 165k/18k; 7 servers 257k/14k; 9 servers 296k/12k; 13 servers 460k/8k. Reads scale because they are served locally without atomic broadcast. — [ZooKeeper paper, USENIX ATC 2010](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/atc10/tech/full_papers/Hunt.pdf)
- etcd write throughput (3 members): 583 writes/s at 1.6 ms with one client; 44,341/s at 22 ms with 1,000 clients to the leader; 50,104/s at 20 ms spread over members. Minimum request time is the member RTT (up to about 400 ms cross-continent) plus `fdatasync` (about 10 ms on spinning disk, often under 1 ms on SSD); batching is what makes high throughput possible. — [etcd performance](https://etcd.io/docs/v3.6/op-guide/performance/)
- etcd defaults: heartbeat 100 ms, election timeout 1,000 ms; heartbeat about 0.5-1.5x RTT; election timeout at least 10x RTT; maximum 50,000 ms for global clusters; "very sensitive to disk latencies" (slow fsync causes missed heartbeats and leader loss). — [etcd tuning](https://etcd.io/docs/v3.6/tuning/)

**Named incidents for "where it breaks"**
- Cloudflare, 2 November 2020: a switch entered a partial-failure state for six minutes (LACP and BGP up, data plane dropping); in a 3-node etcd cluster, node 1 could not reach node 3 but node 2 could reach both; node 1 kept starting elections, node 2 kept voting for node 3, votes tied, and etcd went effectively read-only. A cluster manager promoted a synchronous PostgreSQL-style replica (no data loss), but a defect forced rebuilding all replicas, overloading the auth database's primary. Impact: 6 hours 33 minutes, API success as low as 75%, dashboard up to 80x slower. Cloudflare called it a Byzantine failure; a postscript notes it is better described as an omission fault. The article does not mention PreVote or CheckQuorum. — [Cloudflare, A Byzantine failure in the real world](https://blog.cloudflare.com/a-byzantine-failure-in-the-real-world/)
- NATS JetStream 2.12.1 (Jepsen, 8 Dec 2025): JetStream uses Raft but by default flushes to disk every two minutes while acknowledging immediately; a simulated power loss lost 131,418 of 930,005 messages; a single OS crash plus pauses caused lost acknowledged writes and persistent split brain; corruption of `.blk` files on a minority of nodes lost 679,153 of 1,367,069 acknowledged writes. — [Jepsen NATS 2.12.1](https://jepsen.io/analyses/nats-2.12.1)

**Misconceptions**
- "Consensus guarantees there is one leader." Only one per epoch. — DDIA2e p.434-435/PDF 458-459
- "An entry on a majority is committed." Not across terms (Figure 8). — [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)
- "More replicas = more throughput." ZooKeeper writes: 21k/s at 3 servers vs 8k/s at 13. — [ZooKeeper paper](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/atc10/tech/full_papers/Hunt.pdf)
- "Raft is safe, so it is durable." Raft's proofs assume stable storage; NATS's lazy fsync broke that assumption. — [Jepsen NATS 2.12.1](https://jepsen.io/analyses/nats-2.12.1)
- "Raft tolerates any partition." Partial partitions can stall it (Cloudflare 2020). — [Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/a-byzantine-failure-in-the-real-world/)
- "Leaderless EPaxos is strictly better." Tail latency and correctness history say otherwise. — [EPaxos Revisited](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi21/presentation/tollman); [Sutra](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1906.10917)
- "A 4-node cluster tolerates more failures than 3." Both tolerate 1 under majority quorums (2N+1 rule). — [Kafka KRaft ops](https://kafka.apache.org/44/operations/kraft/). Flexible Paxos is the nuance: with even counts you can shrink phase-2 quorums. — [Flexible Paxos](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.06696)

### Inferences
- Teach in this order: single-decree Paxos on one slide of rules (two phases, highest-numbered value rule) with a dueling-proposers demo; Multi-Paxos as "skip phase 1"; Raft as the implementable version with explicit leader rules; Figure 8 as the one subtle rule; read paths; reconfiguration last. Flexible Paxos fits naturally right after Raft, framed as "why majorities in the first place?", and EPaxos as an optional WAN sidebar with its cautionary history.
- Cloudflare 2020 is the canonical motivation for PreVote and CheckQuorum, but the article itself does not name them; the writer should connect them via DDIA's pre-vote paragraph (p.437/PDF 461) and etcd-io/raft's "automatic step-down when quorum is lost", and not attribute the connection to Cloudflare.
- Round-trip budgets worth putting in a table: Multi-Paxos/Raft write = 1 RTT to a majority + fsync; Raft ReadIndex read = 1 RTT (heartbeat round); lease read = 0 RTT but clock-dependent; classic Paxos = 2 RTT; Cassandra LWT v1 = ~4 RTT (third-party figure); EPaxos fast path = 1 RTT to a fast quorum, slow path = 2.

## 5. Coordination services: Chubby, ZooKeeper, etcd, Consul, and Kafka 4.x KRaft; use cases with fencing

### Takeaway
A coordination service is a small, in-memory, consensus-replicated key-value store plus four features that make coordination programmable: atomic CAS-style operations (locks, leases), monotonically increasing version numbers (fencing tokens), session-based failure detection (ephemeral keys), and change notifications (watches). They are built for slow-changing metadata on 3 or 5 nodes, not for data. Every one of them has a default read path weaker than linearizable or an explicit stale-read mode, and every lock built on them is only safe when the protected resource checks a fencing token. Kafka 4.x shows the endpoint of the trend: the coordination layer moved inside the product as a Raft-replicated metadata log.

### Cited Findings

**Category definition (DDIA)**
- ZooKeeper, etcd and Consul look like key-value stores but are not for high write volume or general data; Kubernetes relies on etcd; Spark and Flink HA rely on ZooKeeper. They hold small in-memory data replicated by consensus and are modeled on Chubby. Features: locks and leases, fencing support (zxid and cversion in ZooKeeper, revision in etcd), failure detection via sessions and heartbeats (ZooKeeper ephemeral nodes), and change notifications. Failure detection and notifications don't need consensus. — DDIA2e p.437-438/PDF 461-462
- Use cases: config management (doesn't need consensus; polling a file works), leader/primary election, shard-to-node assignment and rebalancing (Curator helps but it is still hard), outsourcing consensus to 3 or 5 nodes instead of thousands of shards; data changes on minutes-to-hours timescales ("node 10.1.1.23 leads shard 7"), not thousands of times per second (use BookKeeper for fast-changing replicated state). Service discovery usually doesn't need linearizability and benefits from caching; ZooKeeper observers serve non-linearizable reads that survive partitions. — DDIA2e p.439-440/PDF 463-464

**Chubby (Burrows, OSDI 2006)**
- A cell is typically five replicas in different racks; the master needs a majority of votes plus promises (master lease, a few seconds); writes are acknowledged when a majority has them; reads are served by the master alone while its lease is valid; a new master is typically elected in a few seconds (6 s and 4 s observed, up to 30 s). — [Chubby](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/chubby-osdi06.pdf)
- Intended for coarse-grained locks (held hours or days), not fine-grained ones. — [Chubby](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/chubby-osdi06.pdf)
- Fencing: a lock holder obtains a sequencer (`GetSequencer`), passes it to servers, which check it (`CheckSequencer`) against Chubby or the most recent sequencer seen; for services that can't check sequencers, a lock-delay (up to a bound of one minute) keeps a released-by-failure lock unavailable. — [Chubby](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/chubby-osdi06.pdf)
- Sessions: KeepAlive RPCs extend the client lease (default extension 12 s, rising to about 60 s under load); a client grace period of 45 s lets sessions survive master failover; caches are kept consistent by master-sent invalidations piggybacked on KeepAlives; one cell has seen 90,000 clients directly. — [Chubby](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/chubby-osdi06.pdf)
- Chubby uses Paxos; the paper argues a lock service is easier for application developers than a Paxos library. — [Chubby](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/chubby-osdi06.pdf)

**ZooKeeper (Hunt et al., USENIX ATC 2010; current 3.9.7)**
- Two ordering guarantees: linearizable writes, and FIFO client order (a client's requests execute in the order sent). The authors call this "A-linearizability" because Herlihy's definition allows one outstanding operation per client; reads are processed locally at each replica, so reads scale but can be stale. `sync` followed by a read gives a "slow read": the server applies all pending writes before the read. — [ZooKeeper paper](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/atc10/tech/full_papers/Hunt.pdf)
- Lock without herd effect: create `lock-` as EPHEMERAL|SEQUENTIAL; if lowest, you hold it; else watch only the node immediately before yours; on its deletion re-check. — [ZooKeeper paper](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/atc10/tech/full_papers/Hunt.pdf)
- Yahoo! uses in the paper: the crawler Fetching Service (read:write ratio high, over 1,000 ops/s), Katta (leader election, status tracking) and Yahoo! Message Broker. — [ZooKeeper paper](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/atc10/tech/full_papers/Hunt.pdf)
- Current guarantees (3.9 docs): sequential consistency of a client's updates, atomicity, single system image (no older view after failover within a session), reliability, timeliness (view current within tens of seconds or outage detected). Not guaranteed: simultaneous identical views; call `sync()` before reading if B must see what A just wrote. — [ZooKeeper Programmer's Guide](https://zookeeper.apache.org/doc/current/zookeeperProgrammers.html)
- Znodes and sessions: ephemeral nodes die with their session and can't have children; sequential nodes get a 10-digit zero-padded per-parent counter (signed 4-byte int, overflows past 2,147,483,647); session timeout negotiated between 2x and 20x `tickTime`; the cluster (not the client) expires sessions, deleting ephemerals. — [ZooKeeper Programmer's Guide](https://zookeeper.apache.org/doc/current/zookeeperProgrammers.html)
- Watches: standard watches are one-time triggers; a client sees the watch event before the new data; watches aren't delivered while disconnected (an existence watch can miss a create+delete); since 3.6.0 persistent recursive watches stay registered (create, delete, data-change events, not child-change). `zxid` stamps every change (zxid1 < zxid2 means earlier); `cversion` counts child changes; `version` counts data changes. — [ZooKeeper Programmer's Guide](https://zookeeper.apache.org/doc/current/zookeeperProgrammers.html)
- Versions: current release 3.9.7; latest stable 3.8.8; 3.7 EOL since 2 Feb 2024; no 3.10 listed. — [ZooKeeper releases](https://zookeeper.apache.org/releases.html)
- DDIA: strictly, ZooKeeper has linearizable writes but possibly stale reads; etcd v3 gives linearizable reads by default. — DDIA2e p.409/PDF 433
- Curator recipes: Leader Latch and Leader Election; Shared Reentrant Lock, Shared Lock, Shared Reentrant Read Write Lock, Shared Semaphore, Multi Shared Lock; Barrier, Double Barrier; Shared Counter, Distributed Atomic Long; Curator Cache (Path/Node/Tree Cache for pre-3.6 ZooKeeper); Persistent Recursive Watcher, Persistent Node, Persistent TTL Node, Group Member; several queue types. Recipes create parent nodes as CONTAINER nodes. — [Curator recipes](https://curator.apache.org/docs/recipes)

**etcd (Raft; current v3.7.3)**
- Guarantees (v3.6 docs, updated 26 Sep 2025): linearizable by default; `serializable` reads avoid the consensus cost but may be stale; KV operations are durable and strictly serializable; every modification gets one increasing revision (a transaction gets one revision), acting as a logical clock. Watches: ordered by revision, unique, reliable within the history window, resumable from a revision, with progress (bookmark) events; watches are not linearizable and delivery delay is unbounded (about 10 ms typical when healthy); compaction limits history. Timeouts leave outcome unknown; in-flight operations may be aborted during elections without abort responses. — [etcd API guarantees](https://etcd.io/docs/v3.6/learning/api_guarantees/)
- Jepsen etcd 3.4.3 (30 Jan 2020): only strict-serializable behaviour for KV reads, writes and multi-key transactions under pauses, crashes, clock skew, partitions and membership changes. But "etcd locks (like all distributed locks) do not provide mutual exclusion": with 1-3 s lease TTLs, mutual exclusion failed within minutes even in healthy clusters; with 2 s TTLs about 18% of acknowledged updates to a lock-protected set were lost; a lease-expiry bug (issue 11456) granted locks without rechecking lease validity (fixed on master after 3.4.3). Fencing: use the lock key's revision as a monotonic token checked by the resource. Watch from revision 0 starts at current+1, so use revision 1 to read all history. — [Jepsen etcd 3.4.3](https://jepsen.io/analyses/etcd-3.4.3)
- `concurrency` package (client v3.7.3): `Session` wraps a lease kept alive for the client (default TTL 60 s, `WithTTL`); `Mutex` (`Lock`, `TryLock`, `Unlock`, `IsOwner()` returns a comparison usable in a `Txn`, `Header`); `Election` (`Campaign`, `Proclaim`, `Resign`, `Leader`, `Observe`, `Rev` = creation revision of the leader key, `ResumeElection`); `STM` with serializable snapshot isolation by default. — [etcd concurrency package](https://pkg.go.dev/go.etcd.io/etcd/client/v3/concurrency)
- DDIA: with etcd the revision number plus the lease ID serves as a fencing token. — DDIA2e p.376/PDF 400
- Release state: v3.7.0 GA 8 July 2026 (beta 20 May 2026); latest patches v3.7.3, v3.6.16 and v3.5.35 (22 Oct, year inferred as 2026). — [etcd releases](https://github.com/etcd-io/etcd/releases); [Kubernetes blog, etcd 3.7](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2026/07/08/announcing-etcd-3.7/)
- What changed in 3.7 (mark older material): v2store, v2 discovery, v2 requests and the v2 client removed; deprecated `--experimental-*` flags removed; RangeStream RPC for chunked large ranges (planned for Kubernetes v1.37 behind `EtcdRangeStream`); keys-only reads from the in-memory index; LeaseRevoke prioritized under overload and a `FastLeaseKeepAlive` option; client creation no longer blocks (`grpc.WithBlock` ignored); multiarch-only images; built with Go 1.26.4. The announcement cites bbolt v1.5.0 in one place and v1.5.1 in another. — [Announcing etcd v3.7.0](https://etcd.io/blog/2026/announcing-etcd-3.7/)
- Support: etcd 3.5 supported for one year after 3.7.0; 3.4 EOL since 15 May 2026 (search-result summary of the announcement, not verified on the page). — [Kubernetes blog, etcd 3.7](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2026/07/08/announcing-etcd-3.7/)
- jetcd (official Java client): Jepsen (8 Aug 2024) found 0.6.0 through 0.8.2 automatically retried non-idempotent transactions already sent, producing lost updates, G1c cycles, aborted reads and "retrocausal" reads; workaround `ClientBuilder.retryMaxAttempts(0)`; conditional writes do not prevent the aborted-read anomaly. — [Jepsen jetcd 0.8.2](https://jepsen.io/analyses/jetcd-0.8.2). jetcd 0.8.5 changed to "only retry idempotent operations by default" (#1444); latest listed is 0.8.7 (27 Aug, year inferred 2026); a `jetcd-launcher` module starts etcd in a container. — [jetcd releases](https://github.com/etcd-io/jetcd/releases)

**Consul (Raft; docs show v2.1.x as latest)**
- Read modes: `default` (HTTP API default; "strongly consistent in almost all cases", but an old leader can serve stale reads while a new one is elected); `consistent` (leader verifies with a quorum, extra round trip; writes always behave this way); `stale` (any server answers, typically within about 50 ms of the leader with no upper bound; works without quorum; default for DNS). `max_stale`/`discovery_max_stale` fall back to `default` when too far behind, which can amplify overload. Response headers `X-Consul-LastContact`, `X-Consul-KnownLeader`, `X-Consul-Effective-Consistency`. — [Consul consistency modes](https://developer.hashicorp.com/consul/api-docs/features/consistency)

**Kubernetes leases (coordination.k8s.io/v1)**
- Leases implement node heartbeats (one Lease per node in `kube-node-lease`, kubelet updates `spec.renewTime`), leader election for HA kube-controller-manager and kube-scheduler, and API server identity (beta since v1.26). An alpha in v1.36 (`ControllerManagerReleaseLeaderElectionLockOnExit`) releases the lock on exit instead of waiting for TTL. — [Kubernetes leases](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/architecture/leases/)
- client-go leader-election defaults commonly cited as LeaseDuration 15 s, RenewDeadline 10 s, RetryPeriod 2 s were not on the fetched page (unverified; see Gaps).

**Kafka 4.x KRaft as a built-in coordination layer**
- Kafka 4.0 (18 March 2025) was the first major release without ZooKeeper; 3.9 was the last with ZooKeeper mode. Current: 4.4.0 (22 October 2026). — [Kafka 4.0 announcement](https://kafka.apache.org/blog/2025/03/18/apache-kafka-4.0.0-release-announcement/); [Kafka 3.9 announcement](https://kafka.apache.org/blog/2024/11/06/apache-kafka-3.9.0-release-announcement/); [Kafka downloads](https://kafka.apache.org/community/downloads/)
- KRaft (4.4 docs): `process.roles` = broker, controller or both (combined not recommended for critical deployments); controllers form the metadata quorum (one active, others hot standby); metadata lives in `__cluster_metadata-0`; plan 3 or 5 controllers; static quorum via `controller.quorum.voters`, dynamic quorum (KIP-853) via `controller.quorum.bootstrap.servers`, determined by `kraft.version`; add/remove controllers with `kafka-metadata-quorum.sh add-controller` / `remove-controller`. — [Kafka 4.4 KRaft operations](https://kafka.apache.org/44/operations/kraft/)
- Dynamic quorums arrived with KIP-853 in 3.9, which could not convert static quorums; the 4.4 docs describe migrating to dynamic membership via `kraft.version=1` (from 4.1). — [Kafka 3.9 announcement](https://kafka.apache.org/blog/2024/11/06/apache-kafka-3.9.0-release-announcement/); [Kafka 4.4 KRaft operations](https://kafka.apache.org/44/operations/kraft/)
- KRaft's Raft dialect (KIP-595): pull-based (voters and observers send `Fetch` to the leader; fetches double as liveness; divergence handled via `DivergingEpoch`), unlike push-based Raft; RPCs `Vote`, `BeginQuorumEpoch`, `EndQuorumEpoch` (graceful step-down with preferred successors), `Fetch`, `DescribeQuorum`; epoch = term; high watermark = commit index; the leader writes a `LeaderChangeMessage` in its epoch before advancing the high watermark (Raft's current-term commit rule); voters grant votes only to at-least-as-up-to-date logs; randomized election backoff; a leader that loses majority fetches starts an election. — [KIP-595](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/display/KAFKA/KIP-595%3A+A+Raft+Protocol+for+the+Metadata+Quorum)
- Data replication inside Kafka is still ISR-based, not Raft: `acks=all` waits for the current ISR; `min.insync.replicas` defaults to 1; ELR (KIP-966) is on by default for new clusters from 4.1. — [Kafka 4.4 topic configs](https://kafka.apache.org/44/configuration/topic-configs/); [Kafka ELR](https://kafka.apache.org/44/operations/eligible-leader-replicas/)

**Locks with fencing (the protocol)**
- The HBase-style bug (Figure 9-4): a client holding a storage lease pauses (e.g. GC), the lease expires, another client acquires it, the first resumes and writes, corrupting the file. Shutting nodes down (STONITH) doesn't protect against delayed packets. Fencing tokens: the lock service returns a number that increases with each grant (33, then 34); storage rejects any write with a token lower than one already seen. Fencing is permanent, unlike OCC retries. ZooKeeper: zxid or cversion; etcd: revision plus lease ID; Hazelcast `FencedLock`; object stores' conditional writes (S3 conditional writes, Azure conditional headers, GCS preconditions) serve the same purpose. With leaderless replicas, put the token in the timestamp's most significant bits. — DDIA2e p.373-376/PDF 397-400
- Kleppmann separates locks for efficiency (occasional double work is fine) from locks for correctness (need fencing); antirez's reply argues a random token enables check-and-set; the practical point is that fencing requires the protected resource to cooperate. — [Kleppmann 2016](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2016/02/08/how-to-do-distributed-locking.html); earlier round's Redis notes

**Misconceptions**
- "Coordination services are just a highly available config database." They are for small, slow-changing coordination metadata. — DDIA2e p.439-440/PDF 463-464
- "A ZooKeeper/etcd/Consul read is always current." ZooKeeper reads are local; etcd `serializable`; Consul `stale` and `default` caveat. — sources above
- "A distributed lock gives mutual exclusion." Jepsen etcd 3.4.3, 18% lost updates. — [Jepsen etcd 3.4.3](https://jepsen.io/analyses/etcd-3.4.3)
- "Watches are a reliable event stream." ZooKeeper one-shot watches miss events during disconnects; etcd watches are not linearizable and limited by compaction. — sources above
- "Client libraries are transparent." jetcd's retries manufactured anomalies on a correct server. — [Jepsen jetcd 0.8.2](https://jepsen.io/analyses/jetcd-0.8.2)
- "Kafka still needs ZooKeeper." Not since 4.0 (March 2025).

### Inferences
- Present the four primitives (CAS, version/revision, session/ephemeral, watch) as the "coordination instruction set", then show each product's spelling: ZooKeeper `create(EPHEMERAL|SEQUENTIAL)`/`version`/`zxid`/watch; etcd `Txn(If CreateRevision==0)`/revision/lease/watch; Consul KV `cas`/`ModifyIndex`/session/blocking query (Consul KV specifics not fetched); Kubernetes Lease `resourceVersion`/`renewTime`. Leader election and fenced locks then become ten-line recipes over that instruction set.
- KRaft is the best modern argument for DDIA's "outsource consensus to a small group": Kafka now runs that small group itself (3-5 controllers) while partition data keeps ISR replication, so learners see consensus for metadata and a cheaper protocol for bulk data in one product.
- For the course's JVM stack, jetcd's history is a lab in itself: run the same workload with retries on (0.8.2) and with 0.8.5+ defaults or `retryMaxAttempts(0)`, and check with a history checker.

### Gaps
- Curator's current version and its connection-state guidance (SUSPENDED/LOST) were not on the fetched page.
- Consul's KV CAS, sessions and lock semantics, and whether "v2.1.x" in the docs selector corresponds to a Consul 2.x release, were not verified.
- client-go leader-election defaults (15 s/10 s/2 s) are from background knowledge, unverified this session.
- ZooKeeper Observers and read-only mode docs were not fetched (DDIA covers observers).
- Whether ZooKeeper's `sync` + read is strictly linearizable under leader change (sync is not a quorum operation in the 2010 design) is a known debate; I found no primary source this session to settle it, so teach it as "much fresher, as documented", not as a proof.

## 6. Product knobs (as of November 2026): what each setting buys in the model map

### Takeaway
Every major store exposes the consistency trade-off as a per-request or per-table knob, and the defaults are usually not linearizable. The knob vocabulary differs, but each maps to the same three questions: how many copies must acknowledge a write, which copy (or quorum) a read may use, and whether the leader re-checks its leadership before answering.

### Cited Findings

**Cassandra (5.0.x GA; 6.0 alpha with Accord)**
- Consistency levels: ONE, TWO, THREE, QUORUM (n/2+1), ALL, LOCAL_QUORUM, EACH_QUORUM, LOCAL_ONE, ANY (write-only, may be just a hint). W + R > RF gives overlapping replica sets; LWW per column on client/coordinator timestamps. — [Cassandra Dynamo architecture](https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/architecture/dynamo.html)
- Lightweight transactions (`INSERT ... IF NOT EXISTS`, `UPDATE ... IF`) use Paxos and "incur a non-negligible performance cost". — [CQL DML](https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/developing/cql/dml.html) (via earlier round)
- Serial consistency sets the Paxos phase: SERIAL (quorum across DCs, linearizable for LWTs) and LOCAL_SERIAL (within one DC, like LOCAL_QUORUM); ignored for non-conditional statements. — [DataStax serial consistency (3.0 docs)](https://docs.datastax.com/en/cassandra-oss/3.0/cassandra/dml/dmlConfigSerialConsistency.html)
- Paxos v2 (Cassandra 4.1+, `paxos_variant: v2`): third parties say write round trips drop from four to two; Instaclustr cites a promised 50% LWT improvement, but its Cadence benchmark saw no noticeable application gain. — [AxonOps Paxos v2](https://axonops.com/blog/paxos-v2-and-lightweight-transactions/); [Instaclustr Cadence benchmark](https://www.instaclustr.com/blog/cadence-performance-benchmarking/)
- Version state: the "latest" docs page is 5.0 and links "View Latest" to 6.0-alpha2; nav also lists 6.0 and 7.0. — [Cassandra Dynamo architecture](https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/architecture/dynamo.html). As of mid-July 2026 only 6.0-alpha1 was on the download page, with no GA date; Accord (leaderless, strict-serializable multi-partition transactions) is opt-in per table. — [Cassandra 6 status (blog, July 2026)](https://www.youngju.dev/blog/2026-07-17-cassandra-6-accord-alpha1-status.en). Earlier round: `transactional_mode` = off / mixed_reads / full; LOCAL, TWO and THREE unsupported with Accord. — [Cassandra 6 docs preview](https://pmcfadin.github.io/cassandra6-docs-workzone/operators/draft/upgrade/onboarding-to-accord.html) (draft)
- DDIA: Cassandra waits for read repair on quorum reads but wall-clock LWW still breaks linearizability. — DDIA2e p.413/PDF 437

**DynamoDB**
- Tables and LSIs: eventually consistent (default) or strongly consistent (`ConsistentRead=true` on GetItem, Query, Scan); GSIs and Streams are always eventually consistent. Eventually consistent reads cost half. DynamoDB provides read-committed isolation per item. — [DynamoDB read consistency](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazondynamodb/latest/developerguide/HowItWorks.ReadConsistency.html)
- Global tables: MREC (default) replicates asynchronously, typically within a second; same-item concurrent writes in two Regions resolved last-writer-wins by internal timestamp; a strongly consistent read is only current if the item was last written in that Region; RPO = replication delay. MRSC replicates synchronously to at least one other Region before acknowledging; strongly consistent reads on any replica return the latest version; exactly three Regions (three replicas, or two plus a non-readable witness); mode fixed at creation; concurrent cross-Region modifications fail with `ReplicatedWriteConflictException` (retryable); no TTL, no LSIs, no transactions; RPO zero; higher latency set by inter-Region RTT. — [DynamoDB global tables](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazondynamodb/latest/developerguide/V2globaltables_HowItWorks.html)
- MREC transactions are ACID only within the Region where invoked; other Regions can see partial transactions. — [DynamoDB global tables](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazondynamodb/latest/developerguide/V2globaltables_HowItWorks.html)
- MRSC GA on 30 June 2025; 2026 additions include fault-injection testing for MRSC (Region isolation via AWS FIS). — [AWS What's New, June 2025](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2025/06/amazon-dynamo-db-global-tables-multi-region-strong-consistency-generally-available/) (via earlier round); [DynamoDB global tables](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazondynamodb/latest/developerguide/V2globaltables_HowItWorks.html)

**MongoDB (9.0 current)**
- Write concern default `w: "majority"` (exception: `{w:1}` in some arbiter topologies); default read concern `local` can return data later rolled back; causal sessions need majority reads and writes. — [MongoDB defaults](https://www.mongodb.com/docs/manual/reference/mongodb-defaults/)
- `readConcern: "linearizable"`: reflects all majority-acknowledged writes completed before the read started, may wait for concurrent writes to reach a majority; with `w: "majority"`, single-document reads and writes behave linearizably; primary only; filter must identify one document (e.g. `_id` or unique index); unavailable in causal sessions; no `$out`/`$merge`; use `maxTimeMS` or it may block indefinitely; slower than `majority` because the primary confirms with secondaries that it can still acknowledge majority writes. — [MongoDB linearizable read concern](https://www.mongodb.com/docs/manual/reference/read-concern-linearizable/)
- The Java driver 5.14.0 (28 Oct 2026) supports 9.0; 5.12.0 sends `afterClusterTime` on writes in causal sessions. — [mongo-java-driver releases](https://github.com/mongodb/mongo-java-driver/releases) (via earlier round)

**Spanner**
- External consistency (strict serializability) via TrueTime, a distributed clock giving monotonically increasing timestamps across servers; stale reads at an earlier timestamp trade freshness for eventual-like performance without blocking writes. — [Spanner docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/spanner/docs/true-time-external-consistency)
- Commit wait: Spanner waits out the clock-uncertainty interval before returning so later transactions get larger timestamps. — DDIA2e p.424-425/PDF 448-449. The OSDI 2012 paper reports ε averaging about 4 ms (lecture slides; commit wait ≈ 2ε ≈ 10 ms worst case without overlap). — [Princeton COS 418 Spanner slides](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr24/cos418/docs/L17-spanner-pt1.pdf). The paper's "sawtooth from about 1 to 7 ms" wording is `[not re-read]` ([Spanner, OSDI 2012](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi12/technical-sessions/presentation/corbett)).

**Kafka (4.4.0)**
- Producer defaults: `acks=all`, idempotence on unless conflicting configs silently disable it. Topic default `min.insync.replicas=1`; `acks=all` waits only for the current ISR; recommended RF 3, min ISR 2 for transactional topics; `unclean.leader.election.enable=false` by default; ELR on by default for new clusters since 4.1 fixes "last replica standing". — [Kafka 4.4 producer configs](https://kafka.apache.org/44/configuration/producer-configs/); [topic configs](https://kafka.apache.org/44/configuration/topic-configs/); [KafkaProducer javadoc](https://kafka.apache.org/44/javadoc/org/apache/kafka/clients/producer/KafkaProducer.html); [ELR](https://kafka.apache.org/44/operations/eligible-leader-replicas/)
- DDIA cites unclean leader election as the example of trading consensus safety for availability. — DDIA2e p.436/PDF 460

**PostgreSQL (18.6 current; 19 at RC1, 15 Oct 2026)**
- `synchronous_commit`: `off` (no wait for local WAL flush; crash may lose recent commits but stays consistent; max delay 3x `wal_writer_delay`, default 200 ms, so about 600 ms); `local` (local flush only); `remote_write` (standby received and wrote to OS; survives standby PG crash, not OS crash); `on` (default; standby flushed to durable storage); `remote_apply` (standby replayed, so standby queries see it; largest delay). With empty `synchronous_standby_names` only on/off matter. Settable per transaction (`SET LOCAL synchronous_commit TO OFF`). — [PostgreSQL 18 WAL config](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/runtime-config-wal.html)
- Only `remote_apply` gives read-your-writes on a synchronous standby (the "standby query consistency" column). — [PostgreSQL 18 WAL config](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/runtime-config-wal.html)
- PG 19 RC1 announced 15 Oct 2026; earlier report expected GA on 19 November 2026. — [PG versioning](https://www.postgresql.org/support/versioning/) (via earlier round)

**etcd, ZooKeeper, Consul read knobs (summary)**
- etcd: linearizable default; `serializable` per request (etcdctl `--consistency=s`, Go `clientv3.WithSerializable()` `[API names from background knowledge]`). Linearizable vs serializable latency 0.7 vs 0.3 ms (single client). — [etcd API guarantees](https://etcd.io/docs/v3.6/learning/api_guarantees/); [etcd performance](https://etcd.io/docs/v3.6/op-guide/performance/)
- ZooKeeper: local reads by default; `sync()` then read for freshness. — [ZooKeeper Programmer's Guide](https://zookeeper.apache.org/doc/current/zookeeperProgrammers.html)
- Consul: `?stale`, default, `?consistent`. — [Consul consistency](https://developer.hashicorp.com/consul/api-docs/features/consistency)

### Inferences
- A single comparison table for the writer (inferred from the findings above):

| Store | Write durability knob | Read freshness knob | Linearizable path | Default |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cassandra 5.0 | CL (ONE..ALL, LOCAL_QUORUM) | CL | LWT with SERIAL/LOCAL_SERIAL (Paxos) for single-partition CAS; Accord in 6.0 alpha | client-chosen CL; LWW timestamps |
| DynamoDB | always durable on 200 | `ConsistentRead` | strongly consistent read on table/LSI in one Region; MRSC for cross-Region | eventually consistent reads; MREC |
| MongoDB 9.0 | `w` (1, majority) | readConcern local/majority/linearizable/snapshot | `linearizable` + `w:majority`, single doc, primary | `w:majority`, `local` |
| Spanner | n/a (always) | strong vs stale/bounded staleness | strong reads (external consistency) | strong |
| Kafka 4.4 | `acks`, `min.insync.replicas` | n/a (consumers read below HW) | not a register; per-partition total order | `acks=all`, min ISR 1 |
| PostgreSQL 18 | `synchronous_commit`, `synchronous_standby_names` | read primary vs standby | primary reads; `remote_apply` for standby RYW | `on`, async standbys unless named |
| etcd 3.7 | always quorum | linearizable/serializable | default | linearizable |
| ZooKeeper 3.9 | always quorum | local vs `sync`+read | `sync` then read (see caveat) | local reads |
| Consul | always quorum | stale/default/consistent | `consistent` | `default` |

- Teaching angle: each row is a point on PACELC chosen per request, not per product, which retires the "MongoDB is CP" style of labeling.

- Spanner's current production ε values are not published in the docs page fetched.

## 7. Hands-on lab ideas (laptop: Kotlin on JVM 21+, Gradle, Testcontainers or Docker Compose, or pure-Kotlin simulation)

### Takeaway
Every concept above can be made observable with either a deterministic pure-Kotlin simulation (virtual clock, seeded random network) or a small Docker Compose cluster. The best labs end with a number the learner reads off (lost updates, percentage of non-linearizable histories, election downtime, latency ratio) or a checker that prints a counterexample history.

### Cited Findings
- Versions to pin (November 2026): etcd v3.7.3 ([etcd releases](https://github.com/etcd-io/etcd/releases)); jetcd 0.8.7 with idempotent-only retries since 0.8.5 ([jetcd releases](https://github.com/etcd-io/jetcd/releases)); ZooKeeper 3.9.7 ([ZooKeeper releases](https://zookeeper.apache.org/releases.html)); Kafka 4.4.0 ([Kafka downloads](https://kafka.apache.org/community/downloads/)); MongoDB 9.0 ([MongoDB docs](https://www.mongodb.com/docs/manual/reference/read-concern-linearizable/)); PostgreSQL 18.6 ([PG docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/runtime-config-wal.html)); Cassandra 5.0.x ([Cassandra docs](https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/architecture/dynamo.html)); Lincheck 3.7 ([Lincheck](https://github.com/JetBrains/lincheck)).
- Reference behaviours to reproduce: Figure 10-6 quorum race (DDIA2e p.413/PDF 437); Raft election numbers (no randomness over 10 s; 5 ms randomness median 287 ms; 12-24 ms timeouts average 35 ms) ([Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)); etcd lock 18% lost updates with 2 s TTLs ([Jepsen etcd 3.4.3](https://jepsen.io/analyses/etcd-3.4.3)); etcd linearizable 0.7 ms vs serializable 0.3 ms ([etcd performance](https://etcd.io/docs/v3.6/op-guide/performance/)); ZooKeeper write throughput falling with ensemble size ([ZooKeeper paper](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/atc10/tech/full_papers/Hunt.pdf)); Cloudflare partial partition ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/a-byzantine-failure-in-the-real-world/)); jetcd retry anomalies ([Jepsen jetcd](https://jepsen.io/analyses/jetcd-0.8.2)).
- Checker options on the JVM: Lincheck for in-process objects ([Lincheck](https://github.com/JetBrains/lincheck)); for distributed histories the course can port the Wing-Gong/Lowe backtracking idea that Porcupine optimizes ([Porcupine](https://github.com/anishathalye/porcupine)); Elle (Clojure, runs on the JVM) for transactional histories ([Elle](https://github.com/jepsen-io/elle)).
- etcd-io/raft's Ready loop ordering (persist before send) is a ready-made spec for the Kotlin Raft lab's storage interface. — [etcd-io/raft](https://github.com/etcd-io/raft)

### Inferences (lab designs; all are proposals, none executed yet)

**L1. Draw, then check, a linearizable history (pure Kotlin, 45 min).** Learners write a Wing-Gong-style backtracking checker (about 100 lines) for a register with read, write and CAS. Inputs: DDIA Figure 10-4 transcribed as call/return timestamps. Observable: the checker accepts the history minus B's final read and rejects it with B's final read included, printing the partial linearization. Second measurement: checker runtime as concurrency grows from 2 to 8 clients (shows the exponential blow-up that motivates P-compositionality, i.e. per-key partitioning).

**L2. Lincheck on "replicated" in-process registers (Gradle + Lincheck 3.7, 30 min).** Implement three registers: `AtomicReference` (passes), a "leader plus lazily copied follower" that serves reads from the follower (fails: Lincheck prints a non-linearizable interleaving), and a version that reads from the leader (passes). Observable: the counterexample trace. Bridges multicore memory (DDIA p.416) to replication.

**L3. Quorums are not linearizable (pure-Kotlin simulation, 60 min).** Simulate n=3 replicas, w=3 writer, r=2 readers, random per-message delays from a seeded distribution. Record histories and feed them to the L1 checker. Observable: percentage of runs with a Figure 10-6 violation as delay variance grows (expected 0% with zero variance, rising with variance). Fix 1: synchronous read repair; fix 2: read-before-write timestamp (ABD). Observable: violations drop to 0%, and the extra round trips per operation are counted. Stretch: add wall-clock LWW with one replica's clock 5 s ahead and show silent lost writes (ties to Cassandra docs' NTP warning).

**L4. Lamport clocks vs a timestamp oracle (pure Kotlin, 30 min).** Two "shards" (accounts, photos) assign Lamport timestamps; reproduce DDIA Figure 10-10 (private account, photo visible to a stranger) by letting the photo shard's counter lag. Observable: the leaked photo. Fix with a single replicated counter (batch allocation of 1,000 IDs); observable: leak count 0, and IDs skipped after a simulated failover (but none duplicated).

**L5. Paxos by hand, then dueling proposers (pure Kotlin, 45 min).** Implement single-decree acceptors and proposers exactly per Paxos Made Simple. Observable 1: with any message loss or reordering, all learners decide the same value (assert across 10,000 seeded runs). Observable 2: two proposers with no backoff livelock (count rounds; never decides within budget); add randomized backoff or a distinguished proposer and measure rounds-to-decision. This is FLP made visible: safety always holds, termination needs timing.

**L6. Raft in Kotlin over a deterministic simulated network (multi-session, the anchor lab).** Pre-written: virtual clock, seeded network with drop/delay/partition/partial-partition primitives, persistent-storage interface that models fsync (crash loses unsynced data). Learner writes RequestVote, AppendEntries, commit index, no-op on election, ReadIndex reads. Invariant checker after every step: Election Safety, Log Matching, Leader Completeness, State Machine Safety (Raft Figure 3). Experiments with numbers:
- (a) Election downtime vs randomization: fixed timeout (expect repeated split votes), 5 ms jitter, 150-300 ms; compare medians to the paper's 287 ms / 35 ms results.
- (b) Figure 8: disable the "current term only" commit rule and let the fuzzer find a run where a committed entry is overwritten (State Machine Safety violation printed).
- (c) Durability: acknowledge before fsync (NATS-style); crash two of three nodes; count lost acknowledged writes vs zero with fsync-before-ack.
- (d) Partial partition (Cloudflare topology: 1 cannot reach 3, 2 reaches both): count elections per simulated minute and write availability; then add PreVote and CheckQuorum and re-measure.
- (e) Stale read: serve reads from the leader without ReadIndex; partition the old leader; the L1 checker reports a stale read; with ReadIndex, zero violations, latency +1 RTT.
- (f) Stretch: concurrent single-server membership changes from a 4-node cluster across a leader change (2015 bug) with and without the "commit a current-term entry first" fix.

**L7. etcd leader election, lease expiry and a fencing token (Docker Compose 3-node etcd v3.7.3 or jetcd-launcher, Kotlin + jetcd 0.8.7, 60 min).** Two Kotlin workers campaign for leadership (lease TTL 2 s) and append to a shared "ledger" (a PostgreSQL table or an etcd key). Inject a pause longer than the TTL into the leader (`docker pause` on its container or a blocking sleep simulating GC). Observable 1 (no fencing): both workers write while believing they lead; count lost or duplicated updates (Jepsen measured about 18% lost with similar TTLs). Observable 2 (fencing): each write carries the leader key's create revision; the ledger rejects tokens lower than the highest seen (`UPDATE ... WHERE token >= last_token`, or an etcd `Txn` comparing the stored token); lost updates = 0. Observable 3: jetcd retry behaviour, comparing 0.8.2 retries with 0.8.5+ defaults or `retryMaxAttempts(0)` and checking histories.

**L8. Linearizable vs serializable vs stale reads in coordination services (Compose, 45 min).** etcd: measure p50 latency of linearizable vs serializable reads on a laptop (compare with 0.7 vs 0.3 ms); then `docker network disconnect` one follower, write via the majority, read serializable from the isolated follower (stale value returned) and linearizable (request times out or errors). ZooKeeper 3.9.7: same with a follower read vs `sync()`+read. Consul: `?stale` vs default vs `?consistent`, reading `X-Consul-LastContact`. Observable: a staleness table per mode.

**L9. Cost of consensus as cluster size grows (Compose, 30 min).** Benchmark etcd (or ZooKeeper) writes with 1, 3, 5, 7 members on one laptop. Observable: write throughput and latency per size, read throughput per size (expect writes flat or falling, reads rising, echoing ZooKeeper's 21k-to-8k writes as servers go from 3 to 13). Add 20 ms `tc netem` delay on one link to show RTT dominating.

**L10. KRaft quorum math (Compose, 3 controllers + 3 brokers on Kafka 4.4.0, 30 min).** Stop one controller: metadata operations (create topic) still work; stop a second: they fail while existing partitions keep serving produce/consume. Observable: `kafka-metadata-quorum.sh describe --status` epoch and leader changes; time to elect a new active controller. Contrast with partition-level ISR: RF=3, min ISR 2, `acks=all` succeeds with one broker down and fails with two.

**L11. PostgreSQL synchronous_commit ladder (Compose, primary + 1 synchronous standby, 30 min).** With `tc netem` adding 5 ms on the replication link, measure commit latency for off / local / remote_write / on / remote_apply. Then a read-your-writes probe: write on primary, immediately read on standby; count misses per mode (expect misses for all but `remote_apply`).

**L12. LWT vs QUORUM in Cassandra 5.0 (Compose, 3 nodes, 45 min).** 50 concurrent `INSERT ... IF NOT EXISTS` seat bookings: exactly one `[applied]=true`. Measure LWT vs QUORUM write latency (expect a multiple; third parties cite 4 round trips vs 1). Then the same booking with plain QUORUM read-then-write: count double bookings (expect more than zero under contention).

**L13. MongoDB 9.0 read concern under a stale primary (Compose, 3-member replica set, 45 min).** Isolate the primary with `docker network disconnect`; until it steps down it can still accept `w:1` writes and serve `local` reads. Observable: a `local` read returns a value later rolled back; `majority` doesn't return it; `linearizable` blocks then errors at `maxTimeMS`.

- Sequencing advice: L1-L4 before any algorithm; L5-L6 as the algorithm core; L7-L13 as "product knob" labs. L6 is the only multi-session lab; ship it with the simulator and invariant checker pre-written, matching the earlier round's sizing advice for its Raft lab.

### Gaps
- None of these labs has been executed; container start-up time, arm64 image availability for etcd 3.7.3 (etcd 3.7 images are multiarch per the announcement) and ZooKeeper 3.9.7, and the magnitude of observed effects on a laptop are unverified.
- Whether Testcontainers ships a first-party etcd module (versus jetcd-launcher) was not verified.
- Whether `docker pause` reproduces lease-expiry double leadership reliably within a short lab window is untested; Jepsen's results suggest minutes with 1-3 s TTLs.
- Lincheck 3.7's release date and minimum JDK were not stated on the README.
